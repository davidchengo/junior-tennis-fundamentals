BODY POSITION

Coach’s cue

He asks the player to feel body weight move toward the ball and to prepare before it bounces. The first demonstration is a controlled feed, with time to set up.

Why it may help

Moving into a manageable ball can help the player direct the shot forward while keeping the contact and finish balanced.

Caveat

Forward weight transfer is a feel for this feed, not a requirement to step through every forehand. If the ball pulls you sideways, backward, or into an open stance, keep balance and recover.

Coaching and biomechanics cross-check

The video foregrounds forward drive here. USTA’s beginner progression teaches a coordinated “unit turn” of hips and upper body; forehand research describes racket speed as a coordinated result of pelvis, trunk, shoulder, and arm actions. Taken together, these sources support treating the forward transfer as a context-specific cue rather than a universal rule. USTA: unit turn ↗ · Forehand biomechanics review ↗

MOVEMENT & SPIN

Coach’s cue

In the moving drill, he cues: prepare, turn the shoulders, run, stop, then drive through the ball and recover. He then asks the player to keep the forward intent while adding spin and net clearance.

Why it may help

The progression connects preparation and movement to contact, then adds a control goal: send the ball with shape and enough margin to clear the net.

Caveat

“Stop” is a task cue for this drill, not a frozen stance at contact. Keep moving as needed to adjust to the ball; don’t force a large forward step when spacing or recovery would suffer.

Coaching cross-check

USTA describes brushing from low to high as a way to produce topspin and recommends prioritizing control as players learn. That supports adding shape for margin, while the exact amount of spin and forward movement depends on the ball and player. USTA: forehand by level and spin ↗

READ & ADAPT

Coach’s cue

Mouratoglou explains that a full preparation can make a player late against a fast, deep ball. He demonstrates a shorter take-back, then asks the player to read whether the incoming ball is fast or slow and adjust the swing length.

Why it may help

A compact preparation leaves more time to meet a quick ball in front and use its pace, instead of rushing a large backswing.

Caveat

This is an adjustment to time pressure, not a rule to keep every forehand short. Use a fuller preparation when the ball gives you time; on a fast ball, prioritize early recognition, contact, and balance.

Coaching and research cross-check

The research review notes that forehand coordination changes with incoming-ball speed and shot direction, which supports adapting the action to the situation. This is a context-specific coaching choice, not a rejection of a fuller modern forehand when time allows. Reid, Elliott & Crespo, 2013 ↗

ACCELERATION

Coach’s cue

He asks the player to turn, let the hand stay relaxed, and accelerate through the ball. He also has her picture a higher arc over the net and says not to force the shot by trying to hit hard.

Why it may help

A relaxed arm can make it easier to accelerate the racket while preserving a controllable swing path and net margin.

Caveat

“Relax the hand” is not an instruction to flick the wrist or abandon racket-face control. Keep a comfortable grip and judge the result by contact, height, and depth—not effort alone.

Coaching and research cross-check

USTA guidance pairs a lower-to-higher path for topspin with racquet-face control and a grip that is not overly tight. The biomechanics review supports coordinated segment action; it does not establish a deliberate wrist snap as the way to create power. USTA: grip, control, and spin ↗ · Forehand biomechanics review ↗

TRY IT ON COURT

One variable at a time

Start with 6 comfortable feeds. On a slower ball, prepare early and notice whether a small forward move helps you send the ball deep with balance. Then take 6 quicker feeds with a shorter preparation. Keep the same target and compare contact quality, net margin, and recovery. Stop if you feel pain or lose control.
